Should you actually move here?

Is Indianapolis for you?

Indianapolis is the rare city that gives you the full major-metro package, an airport that works, academic medicine, three pro teams, a symphony and a serious art museum, without charging major-metro prices for any of it. What it does not give you is landscape. Central Indiana is flat, and the people who are happy here are the ones who wanted a city, not a view.

✓
You'll love it here if…
  • You want a whole city, not a slice of one. Colts, Pacers and Fever all play here, Newfields runs a 152-acre art-and-garden campus, the Indianapolis Symphony has its own hall on Monument Circle, and Mass Ave and Fountain Square carry the independent restaurants. That is a metro's worth of institutions at a Range 1 budget.
  • You are going to be on planes a lot. Indianapolis International sits twelve miles from downtown, roughly fifteen minutes outside rush hour, with about 55 nonstop destinations on eleven airlines and an Aer Lingus route to Dublin that opens Europe without a domestic connection. Travelers have voted it first in its size category in the Airports Council International passenger survey fourteen years running.
  • You want academic medicine without a coastal cost of living. IU Health Methodist is a Level I trauma center and the flagship of Indiana's academic health system, tied to the Indiana University School of Medicine. Its medical center was rated High Performing in geriatrics, cancer, gastroenterology and neurology in the 2025–26 U.S. News edition, and Ascension St. Vincent and Community Health Network give you somewhere else to go for a second opinion.
  • Social Security is the backbone of your income. Indiana exempts Social Security benefits from state income tax entirely, and the flat state rate on everything else is 2.95%, among the lowest flat rates anywhere. If most of your income is Social Security, the Indiana tax bill is close to nothing.
✗
Skip Indianapolis if…
  • You need terrain out your back door. This is the weakest thing about the city and it is not close. Outdoor recreation scores a 3 of 10. Central Indiana is flat farmland, there is no coast, no mountains, and no big water. Eagle Creek Park and the Monon Trail are genuinely good and genuinely used, but the nearest real hills are Brown County, over an hour south, and everything beyond that is a flight.
  • You are done with winter. January averages 28°F, roughly 25 inches of snow falls in a normal year, and the sun is out about 52% of the time. February in Indianapolis is grey for weeks at a stretch. Plenty of retirees here are snowbirds for exactly that reason.
  • You want to give up the car. The walkable pockets are real but small: Mass Ave, Fountain Square, Broad Ripple, the Carmel Arts & Design District. Outside them the metro is built around arterials and parking lots, and the bus network will not replace a car for most retirees.
  • You expected all retirement income to be untaxed. Social Security is exempt, but pension, 401(k) and IRA withdrawals are taxed at the flat state rate with no general retiree exclusion, and every Indiana county adds its own income tax on top. Marion County charges 2.02%; Hamilton County, which covers Carmel and Fishers, charges about half that.
The character of the place

A full-size city that never priced itself up.

Indianapolis was laid out in 1821 on a flat square of central Indiana, by a surveyor who had worked on Washington, DC, which is why there is a circle at the middle with four diagonal avenues running off it. Nothing about the site was special. There is no harbor, no fall line, no mountain pass. The city exists because the state decided it should, and it grew into one of the fifteen biggest cities in the United States on freight, insurance and manufacturing. That origin explains the thing retirees notice first: Indianapolis has a major city's institutions, an academic medical center, a symphony, a serious art museum, three professional teams and an airport with transatlantic service, sitting on land that never became expensive.

The whole metro is organized by a ring road and a county line. Marion County is the city proper, 887,000 people, and it holds the downtown, the medical campus, the stadiums and the walkable neighborhoods. North of it, Hamilton County holds Carmel, Zionsville and Fishers, which is where most retirees who choose Indianapolis actually end up. That is not a compromise so much as a different product: Carmel has a concert hall, a hundred-odd roundabouts and a rail-trail through the middle of it, and it is one of the most deliberately built suburbs in America. The retirees who stay in the city cluster on the north side, in Broad Ripple and Meridian Kessler, where the housing stock is prewar and the canal towpath is a block away.

The culture has grown faster than the reputation. Newfields, the campus that holds the Indianapolis Museum of Art, runs 152 acres of gardens and a sculpture park alongside the collection. The Eiteljorg carries Native American and Western art that most cities its size have no equivalent for. On the near south side, Garfield Park has been a city park since the 1870s and still carries Kessler's 1916 sunken garden. Mass Ave is a genuine restaurant row. And then there is the month of May, which is not really about motorsport for most residents so much as it is a city-wide civic season that ends with 300,000 people at a racetrack on the west side. What Indianapolis has, and what its reputation has not caught up to, is depth at a price nobody expects to pay for it.

On the trail that connects the retiree map

The Monon runs on the old railbed from Broad Ripple north through Nora and into Carmel, flat and paved the whole way. It is the one line on the map that links every neighborhood a retiree here would actually shop, and on a Saturday morning it is full of them.

Where to live
Reading the numbers here: the $234K figure in the stat card is the Indianapolis city-limits median, and it is pulled down by large parts of Marion County that retirees do not shop. The four areas below are where they actually buy, three of them outside the city line, and each carries its own local price. Expect the realistic number to be roughly double the citywide one.

Four ways to live here, and three are north.

The retiree map in Indianapolis runs up the north side and over the Hamilton County line. The four below span that range, from the deliberately built suburb to the prewar city neighborhood, with the Monon Trail as the thread that connects them. Pricing reflects May 2026 estimates and varies by municipality, block, and lot.

Carmel
Built · Amenity-rich · Very safe
The most deliberately designed suburb in the metro: a walkable Arts & Design District, the Palladium concert hall, the Monon running through the middle of it, and roundabouts instead of traffic lights nearly everywhere. Consistently near the top of national best-places-to-live lists, with crime rates to match. Hamilton County's income tax is also roughly half Marion County's, which is a real annual saving. Typical value around $525K.
Zionsville
Village · Historic · Premium
A brick-paved main street, mature trees, and a small-town scale that Carmel traded away for amenities. Boone County rather than Hamilton, twenty-five minutes to the medical campus. The premium end of the retiree map here and the one that feels least like a suburb. Typical value around $689K.
Fishers
Newer · Practical · Value
Carmel's more affordable neighbor on the same side of the county, with the same low Hamilton County tax rate, a rebuilt downtown around the Nickel Plate district, and IU Health's Fishers hospital in town. The pick for people who want the north-suburban package without the Carmel premium. Typical value around $425K.
Broad Ripple & Meridian Kessler
In-city · Prewar · Walkable
The two north-side city neighborhoods that hold their own. Broad Ripple is the village: independent restaurants, the canal towpath, the southern trailhead of the Monon. Meridian Kessler next door is quieter and grander, with 1920s houses on wide streets. Marion County taxes and Marion County schools, in exchange for a village you can actually walk into. Range: roughly $400K–$500K, with the largest Meridian Kessler houses well above.
Healthcare: the reason the value holds up

Indiana's academic medicine, on a downtown campus.

🏥
IU Health Methodist Hospital · Indiana University School of Medicine
Methodist is a Level I trauma center and the flagship of Indiana's academic health system, tied to the Indiana University School of Medicine, with subspecialty depth in cardiovascular and thoracic surgery, neurosurgery, neurocritical care and transplant. IU Health Medical Center was rated High Performing in geriatrics, cancer, gastroenterology and neurology in the 2025–26 U.S. News Best Hospitals edition, and geriatrics is the one that matters most here. IU Health is currently building a consolidated downtown hospital and campus, due to open in 2027, that will combine Methodist and University hospitals. Ascension St. Vincent and Community Health Network run competing systems across the metro, so a second opinion does not mean leaving town. This is not Cleveland Clinic, and the profile does not claim it is, but it is a real academic center on a fifteen-minute drive from the north-side neighborhoods.

Visit Before You Decide

Travelers have voted Indianapolis International first in its size category fourteen years running in the Airports Council International passenger survey, which is a strange thing to lead with about a city until you land there and understand it: twelve miles out, one terminal, no train between concourses, downtown in about fifteen minutes. That efficiency is the whole Indianapolis argument in miniature. The city is bigger than its reputation and easier than its size. The flatness is real and you will notice it on the drive in.

The visit that tells you something happens north of downtown. Stay in Carmel or Broad Ripple, then spend a day each in Zionsville and Fishers, because these four are the actual choice and they are genuinely different from one another in ways no listing photo conveys. Drive from whichever one you like best down to IU Health Methodist at 8am on a weekday and time it honestly. Walk the Monon in the morning and again after dinner. Come in February if you can stand it, because that is the month that decides this one. Test the daily routine, not the highlight reel.
