Should you actually move here?

Is Casper for you?

Casper is a narrow, well-defined offer: the lightest state tax burden available anywhere in the country, costs that sit in the lowest budget tier, a mountain with a ski area on it eleven miles from downtown, and a safety picture most cities its size cannot match. What it does not offer is convenience. One nonstop flight, almost nothing within walking distance, and a winter that runs long and windy. The retirees who thrive here already know they want a quiet, outdoor, low-tax life and are willing to drive for everything else.

✓
You'll love it here if…
  • Taxes are the number you optimize. Wyoming has no state income tax at all. Not on Social Security, not on pension income, not on IRA or 401(k) withdrawals, not on capital gains. No estate tax, no inheritance tax. Property tax runs about 0.53% of value, and the state sales tax is 4% before county add-ons.
  • You want the mountain close, not scenic. Casper Mountain rises to 8,000 feet directly south of town, with 50-plus miles of trail, the Hogadon Basin ski area eleven miles from downtown, and a Nordic center with groomed winter trails. The paved River Trail runs roughly ten miles along the North Platte through the middle of the city.
  • A fixed income needs to stretch. The typical home runs $314,000 and a full retiree budget lands around $4,800 to $5,900 a month, which is Range 1, the lowest budget tier. The average commute is about 17 minutes, and there is effectively no traffic.
  • Quiet reads as a feature, not a flaw. Casper is a city of roughly 59,000 people where crime sits well below the national average and neighborhood selection is not the fraught exercise it is in a big metro. The trade is obvious and worth naming: nothing here is open late.
✗
Skip Casper if…
  • You fly often. This is the hardest constraint on the page. Casper/Natrona County International has one nonstop route, to Denver, on one airline. Everything else is a connection, or a four-hour drive to Denver International. Grandchildren on either coast means a full travel day, twice.
  • You want to walk to things. Outside a compact downtown core around David Street Station, Casper is a car-first grid. Groceries, the hospital, the trailheads, and the pharmacy all assume you drive. Giving up the keys later in life is a genuinely harder proposition here than in a walkable city.
  • Wind and winter wear on you. January averages around 26 degrees, roughly 76 inches of snow falls in a normal year, and the high-plains wind is close to constant. Locals treat it as background noise. Visitors from milder places often do not.
  • You need medical depth or a big cultural scene. Banner Wyoming Medical Center covers regional needs well and holds the state's only Level II trauma verification, but subspecialty and academic-tier care usually means Denver or Salt Lake City. The arts calendar is real and small: a symphony, an art museum, a rodeo, a handful of festivals.
The character of the place

A crossroads town that kept its bargain.

Casper exists because of a river crossing. The Oregon, California, and Mormon Pioneer trails all forded the North Platte here, the Pony Express ran through, and Fort Caspar went up to guard the ferry. Oil arrived in the 1890s and never entirely left, which is why a city of about 59,000 people has a downtown built to a grander scale than its population suggests, and why its fortunes still rise and fall with a commodity price. It is the second largest city in Wyoming, which in a state of 590,000 people means it functions as central Wyoming's hospital, airport, symphony, and shopping trip all at once.

The tax picture is why most retirees look at Wyoming in the first place. There is no state income tax of any kind, which means Social Security, pensions, IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, and investment income arrive untouched by the state. Property tax is low as well, roughly 0.53% of value, and there is no estate or inheritance tax. One honest catch is worth knowing before you plan around it: Wyoming's headline 50% senior property tax break requires 25 years of Wyoming property tax payments, so a retiree arriving from out of state does not qualify. The broader 25% homeowner exemption on a primary residence is the one newcomers can actually use, and it carries application deadlines and a legislative sunset, so it should be checked for the year you buy rather than assumed.

What makes Casper more than a tax argument is the ten minutes it takes to leave it. Casper Mountain rises straight out of the south side of town to 8,000 feet, carrying 50-plus miles of trail, a small ski area at Hogadon Basin, groomed Nordic tracks, and Garden Creek Falls at its base. The North Platte runs through the middle of the city as blue-ribbon trout water with a paved trail beside it. That combination, cheap housing, low taxes, real outdoor access, and a safety record well above the national average, is what the city actually sells. The wind and the winter are the price, and everyone here will tell you so within ten minutes of meeting you.

On a downtown built for a bigger city

Oil money left Casper a downtown scaled well past its population. The old Wyoming National Bank, a white concrete dome under a 177-foot tower, went up in 1968 to a design by Casper architect Harold Engstrom and still marks the center of a city of 59,000. The boom-and-bust arithmetic of the last century is legible in the skyline, and so is the civic ambition behind it.

What life actually looks like

The River Trail
Roughly ten paved miles along the North Platte, flat and easy, with benches, bridges, and a kayak launch. Park at the Tate Pumphouse near the middle and walk in either direction. This is the everyday version of outdoor Casper, the one you use in ordinary shoes.

The NIC, downtown
The Nicolaysen Art Museum, Wyoming's largest art museum, in a converted downtown warehouse. Rotating contemporary and Western shows, an active class schedule, and NIC Fest each June, the longest-running downtown festival in the city.

Fishing the Platte
The tailwater below Grey Reef, about 40 minutes southwest, is blue-ribbon trout water with a national reputation among fly fishers. Guides run trips year round. Closer in, Edness Kimball Wilkins State Park east of town has flat river-edge trails and heavy bird traffic.

5:00 PM
David Street Station
The downtown gathering space: summer farmers market, free concerts, movie nights, and a public ice rink in winter. First Thursday brings the downtown art walk from May through September, with galleries, live music, and food.

Wyoming Symphony
A full symphony orchestra in a city of 59,000, playing a downtown season of classical and pops programs. The Ford Wyoming Center handles the touring acts, with about 8,000 seats.

Twenty minutes to the Bridle Trail and Garden Creek Falls at Rotary Park, or further up to the Casper Mountain trail system, 50-plus miles of it. In winter the same drive gets you to Hogadon Basin for downhill or the Trails Center for groomed Nordic tracks.

The National Historic Trails Interpretive Center on the bluff above town, then Fort Caspar Museum at the old river crossing. Two hours, and the reason the city is here at all makes sense.

Where to live
Reading the numbers here: Casper is unusual among the cities profiled here in that its neighborhoods sit close together on price. The $314K citywide median is a fair guide almost everywhere, and the retiree-favored southwest side prices within a few thousand dollars of it. What varies is age of construction, lot size, and how far you are from the mountain, not the number on the listing.

Four sides of a small city.

Casper is compact enough that no choice here strands you: the far corners are fifteen minutes apart. The real decision is proximity to the mountain, to downtown, or to the river, and how new you want the house. Pricing reflects 2026 estimates and varies by block, condition, and lot.

Paradise Valley
Southwest · Scenic · Golf
The southwest quarter, tucked under the mountain with the best views in the city and mature landscaping that took fifty years to grow. Mostly single-family homes built between 1970 and 1999, largely owner-occupied, with golf and the west end of the River Trail close by. The conventional retiree choice, and the one most locals name first. Median: roughly $316K, effectively level with the citywide figure.
Central Casper & the Old Yellowstone District
Downtown · The walkable pocket · Historic
The one part of the city where errands on foot are realistic: David Street Station, the Nicolaysen Art Museum, restaurants, galleries, and the First Thursday art walk are all within a few blocks. Housing is older and more varied, from early-century bungalows to loft conversions in the Old Yellowstone District. Inventory is thin and priced more by condition than by block.
East Casper
Value · Recreation · Easy access
The affordable entry point, with a mix of established and newer construction, quick interstate access, and golf and recreation close at hand. Also the side of town nearest Banner Wyoming Medical Center and its second campus, which matters more to some buyers than to others. Generally the value end of the city, running below the citywide median.
Mills & Bar Nunn
Adjacent towns · Space · Lower cost
Two small incorporated towns on the edges: Mills sits west along the river with direct trail and boat access, and Bar Nunn spreads north of the city on the old Wardwell airfield, where the main street follows a former runway. Both trade proximity to downtown for more lot and a lower entry price. The cheapest way into the Casper market, with longer drives to everything.
Healthcare: solid regionally, thin on subspecialty

One serious hospital, and a drive for the rest.

🏥
Banner Wyoming Medical Center
Wyoming's largest hospital, with 249 beds across two Casper campuses, and the only facility in Wyoming verified by the American College of Surgeons as a Level II Trauma Center. It works as central Wyoming's referral hub, serving 11 counties and more than 250,000 people with heart, stroke, and trauma care, and it anchors a University of Wyoming family medicine residency. The honest limit: this is strong regional medicine, not academic-tier subspecialty care. Complex oncology, transplant, and rare-disease treatment generally route to Denver, about four hours south, or Salt Lake City. Retirees managing a serious ongoing condition should price that drive into the decision.

Visit Before You Decide

Wyoming takes no income tax of any kind, and Casper is the version of that deal with an 8,000-foot mountain attached to it. The arithmetic is genuinely compelling on paper. What a spreadsheet cannot tell you is whether the wind bothers you, whether a city of 59,000 feels peaceful or small by the fourth day, and whether one nonstop flight is a quirk or a dealbreaker. Those are visit questions, and they answer themselves quickly once you are here.

Come in a shoulder season, when the weather still has an opinion. Most retirees land in Paradise Valley on the southwest side, downtown near the Old Yellowstone District, or the value blocks of East Casper, and all three are worth an afternoon each. Drive from a rental to Banner Wyoming Medical Center and time it, walk the River Trail on a windy morning rather than a still one, and see how far the nearest grocery run actually is. Test the daily routine, not the highlight reel.
