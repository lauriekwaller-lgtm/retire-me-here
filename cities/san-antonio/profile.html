Should you actually move here?

Is San Antonio for you?

San Antonio is a big city that behaves like an old one. It has depth most retirees do not expect from Texas: two UNESCO designations, a teaching hospital, a real airport, and a food culture with three hundred years behind it. It also has a summer that runs from June into September and a walkability story confined to a handful of neighborhoods. The people who thrive here treat the heat as a season to plan around, the way northerners plan around February.

✓
You'll love it here if…
  • Taxes shape your budget. Texas levies no state income tax on Social Security, pensions, IRA or 401(k) withdrawals. The general homestead exemption removes $100,000 from school district taxable value, and turning 65 adds a further exemption plus a permanent freeze on the school portion of the bill.
  • You want a serious airport without a megacity. San Antonio International sits eight miles from downtown, with twelve airlines and nonstop service to 48 destinations across the U.S., Mexico, and Canada. Anything not served nonstop is a connection, or a short hop to the Dallas and Houston hubs.
  • Culture is the reason you move somewhere. The Alamo and the four working missions south of it are a World Heritage Site, and San Antonio is one of only two U.S. cities named a UNESCO Creative City of Gastronomy. Add the River Walk, the Tobin Center, the McNay, the Witte, Fiesta every April, and a Mexican-American culture that is the mainstream here rather than a district.
  • Academic medical depth matters to you. University Hospital is the Level I trauma center for South Texas and the teaching hospital of UT Health San Antonio, whose Mays Cancer Center carries National Cancer Institute designation. Methodist Hospital ranked among the top ten in Texas in the 2025-26 U.S. News rankings.
✗
Skip San Antonio if…
  • Summer heat is a deal-breaker. July and August run mid-90s with triple digits routine and enough humidity to make it count. June through September is an indoor season with early-morning errands, and that is four months, not four weeks. This is the single most common reason people leave.
  • You want to walk everywhere. Walkability here is pocketed, not citywide. The River Walk, the Pearl, King William, and the Broadway corridor through Alamo Heights are genuinely walkable. Outside them the city is a car-dependent sprawl of loops and access roads.
  • Trail access is your priority. The Mission Reach and Government Canyon are real, but the Hill Country starts 30 to 45 minutes north and the terrain in town is flat. Retirees who organize their week around hiking generally do better elsewhere.
  • Citywide crime statistics will unsettle you. The metro-wide numbers are poor and they are what search results will show you. The retiree-target municipalities are a different picture entirely, but you have to choose deliberately rather than buy on a map, and some people never get comfortable with that gap.
The character of the place

An old city, in a state that mostly builds new ones.

San Antonio was founded in 1718, which makes it older than the United States and considerably older than the Texas most people picture. It exists because of the river. Five Spanish missions went up along a twenty-mile stretch of it, four of them still hold services, and the fifth is the Alamo. Three centuries later that same water runs a story below street level through downtown as the River Walk, and it is still the axis the city organizes itself around. In 2015 UNESCO inscribed the mission chain as a World Heritage Site, the only one in Texas. Two years later it named San Antonio a Creative City of Gastronomy, one of only two in the United States. Not many American cities carry one of those. This one carries both, for the same reason: it never stopped being what it was.

The financial case is the plainest part of the story. Texas taxes no retirement income of any kind, and the over-65 school tax freeze locks the largest component of a property tax bill in the year you qualify. That matters because Bexar County property tax rates are high, near 1.8% effective in much of the city. The arithmetic still lands in the retiree's favor for anyone drawing meaningful pension or IRA income, and it lands harder every year the freeze holds. San Antonio International, eight miles from downtown with twelve carriers, is the other piece of infrastructure that shapes daily life: family visits are a direct flight, not a project.

What the money buys is a city with real institutional weight. University Hospital and UT Health San Antonio anchor the medical center on the northwest side. The Tobin Center programs the San Antonio Philharmonic, Ballet San Antonio, and Opera San Antonio out of the old municipal auditorium on the river. The Pearl, a decommissioned brewery north of downtown, turned into the city's best food district and its best farmers market. And the culture underneath all of it is Mexican-American, not as heritage display but as the ordinary texture of the place: the food, the language, the calendar, the eleven days every April when Fiesta effectively closes the city.

On the missions, and what they still are

Four of the five are working parish churches, not ruins. Mass is said at Concepción on a Sunday morning much as it has been since the 1750s. The World Heritage plaque sits at a trailhead, and the trail is eight paved miles you can ride before the heat comes up.

Where to live
Reading the numbers here: San Antonio's city limits cover more than 500 square miles, and the citywide median above (~$251K) averages across all of it. Retirees concentrate in a small number of areas, several of which are independent municipalities with their own police forces and their own price floors. The four below span that range and each lists its own local figure.

Four San Antonios, depending on you.

The choice here is unusually consequential, because the gap between the retiree-target areas and the citywide averages is wide on both price and safety. One practical note that applies everywhere in the metro: this is flash flood country, so check a property's position relative to the creeks and drainage before you fall for the lot. Pricing reflects 2026 estimates and varies by municipality, block, and lot.

Alamo Heights / Olmos Park
Inner-loop · Walkable · Established
The closest thing San Antonio has to a walkable prestige address. Alamo Heights is an independent municipality entirely surrounded by the city, with its own police force and a Broadway corridor of restaurants, galleries, and the McNay a few minutes away. Adjacent Olmos Park is smaller, quieter, and equally self-governing. Both are minutes from Brackenridge Park and the medical corridor. Alamo Heights runs around $700K; Olmos Park $730K and up.
Terrell Hills
Exclusive · Large lots · Quiet
The enclave next door to Alamo Heights, and the most expensive of the inner-loop municipalities. Big lots, mature oaks, its own small police department, and almost no through traffic. Fort Sam Houston and the Broadway restaurants are both a few minutes out. The pick for buyers who want space and quiet without leaving the loop. Typical value around $880K.
Stone Oak
North side · Newer build · Value
Master-planned north San Antonio, up against the Hill Country escarpment. Newer construction, gated sections, medical offices and Methodist Hospital Stone Oak close by, and the most house per dollar of the retiree-target areas. The tradeoff is the commute: downtown and the missions are 25 to 30 minutes south, and nothing here is walkable. Typical value around $525K.
King William Historic District
Historic · Walkable · Urban
Victorian mansions built by German merchants on the river just south of downtown, and the most genuinely urban of the four retiree-target areas here. You can walk to the River Walk, the Blue Star arts complex, and the Southtown restaurants without a car. It is inside city limits, so the safety picture is neighborhood-specific rather than municipality-wide. Range: roughly $400K–$600K.
Healthcare: an academic center, not just a hospital

A teaching hospital, a cancer designation, and real depth behind both.

🏥
University Hospital · UT Health San Antonio
University Hospital is the Level I trauma center for South Texas and the primary teaching hospital of UT Health San Antonio, whose Mays Cancer Center holds National Cancer Institute designation. Methodist Hospital ranked among the top ten hospitals in Texas in the 2025-26 U.S. News rankings, and Methodist Stone Oak puts a strong facility on the north side where many retirees actually live. Brooke Army Medical Center at Fort Sam Houston adds a further layer for military retirees. The depth here is real for nearly everything a retiree will face. For the narrowest tier of complex specialty care, Houston's Texas Medical Center is a three-hour drive or a short flight, and that is the honest limit of the local picture.

Visit Before You Decide

Everyone comes for the River Walk and the Alamo, and both deserve it. What surprises people is what sits south of them: four more Spanish missions, still holding services, strung along eight paved miles of river trail, the whole chain a UNESCO World Heritage Site and the only one in Texas. The thing a visit settles is the summer: the heat here is a season with its own rhythm, and you will only know how you handle it by standing in it.

Pick your base by which version of the city you think you want. Alamo Heights and Olmos Park for walkable and established, Terrell Hills for quiet and green, Stone Oak for newer and roomier up north, King William for genuinely urban. Drive from whichever one you choose to University Hospital and the UT Health medical center in weekday traffic, do a grocery run at 2pm in August, and walk the block at night. Test the daily routine, not the highlight reel.
